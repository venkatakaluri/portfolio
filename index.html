Ford GRC and Operational Risk Platform

Client: Ford Motor Company
Duration: August 2022 - Present
Role: Senior UI/UX Designer
Team Size: 8+ Designers, 20+ Engineers

Challenge

Ford's Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) team needed a modern platform to manage organizational risk and compliance workflows. The system needed to serve different user types—risk officers, control owners, and compliance managers—each with different workflows, permissions, and information needs. The platform required integration with Camunda workflow engine and Snowflake data services while maintaining enterprise-grade reliability and accessibility.

Approach

I started by conducting stakeholder interviews with risk officers, control owners, and IT teams to understand workflow requirements, pain points, and data needs. Created user personas for each role type. Developed wireframes mapping complex workflows including RCSA assessments, issue tracking, control execution, and KRI monitoring.

Established a comprehensive design system with 80+ reusable components ensuring consistency across the platform. Implemented WCAG 2.1 AA accessibility standards from the beginning, including color contrast compliance, keyboard navigation, and screen reader support. Collaborated closely with engineering teams on design-to-code translation and technical feasibility.

Key Deliverables

  • Role-based dashboards for RCSA, Issues & Action Management, Controls, and KRIs
  • 80+ reusable UI components and design patterns
  • Design tokens for colors, spacing, typography, and interactive states
  • Accessibility audit and WCAG 2.1 AA compliance documentation
  • Responsive design supporting desktop, tablet, and mobile breakpoints
  • Interactive prototypes demonstrating complex workflows
  • Design system documentation and component library

Outcomes & Impact

  • Reduced design-to-development time by 40% through component reusability
  • Achieved 100% WCAG 2.1 AA accessibility compliance
  • Established consistent design language across all workflows and teams
  • Improved platform usability enabling faster risk assessments and compliance tracking
  • Design system scaled to support 8+ product teams and 20+ engineers
  • Established design culture and accessibility awareness across teams

Key Learnings

Enterprise GRC platforms require deep understanding of business workflows and regulatory requirements. Accessible design isn't an afterthought—it must be baked into design process from day one. Design systems are most valuable when they include comprehensive documentation and team education, not just component libraries.

Enterprise Design System Architecture

Scope: Multi-Platform Design System
Team Impact: 8+ Product Teams, 20+ Engineers
Role: Design System Architect
Duration: 2022-2024

Challenge

Multiple product teams working independently were creating inconsistent UI patterns, duplicating components, and making design-to-development inefficient. Each team had their own color palettes, button styles, spacing conventions, and interaction patterns. This resulted in fragmented user experiences and wasted development effort rebuilding common components.

Approach

Audited existing UI patterns and components across all products. Identified 30+ commonly repeated components and established baseline design patterns. Created comprehensive design system with consistent token values for color, spacing, typography, and interactive states. Organized components using atomic design methodology enabling progressive complexity from atoms to organisms.

Developed extensive documentation including component specifications, usage guidelines, accessibility requirements, and code examples. Created living documentation in Figma enabling teams to access latest component specifications and contribute improvements. Established governance process for component evolution and versioning.

Key Deliverables

  • 80+ reusable UI components (buttons, inputs, cards, tables, modals, etc.)
  • Design tokens for colors, spacing, typography, shadows, and animations
  • Figma component library with variants for different states
  • Component documentation with usage guidelines and code examples
  • Accessibility specifications for each component type
  • Responsive design patterns and breakpoint guidelines
  • Design system governance and contribution process

Outcomes & Impact

  • Reduced design-to-development time by 40% through component reusability
  • Eliminated duplicate component development across teams
  • Established consistent user experience across all products
  • Improved design system adoption rate to 85% across teams
  • Reduced maintenance burden through centralized component management
  • Enabled faster onboarding for new designers joining teams
  • Created foundation for future product scaling and international expansion

Key Learnings

Design systems succeed when teams see them as enabling their work, not constraining it. Comprehensive documentation is as important as the components themselves. Design system is living artifact—it must evolve with products and team needs. Governance and contribution process are critical for system health and adoption.

Mobile-Native iOS and Android Application Design

Platforms: iOS and Android
Users: 2.5M+ Investors
App Rating: 4.8/5 Stars
Focus: Native Experience Design

Challenge

Users expect native mobile experiences that leverage platform conventions, gestures, and design patterns. iOS and Android have different interaction paradigms, navigation patterns, and design languages. Creating platform-native experiences while maintaining brand consistency and feature parity across platforms is challenging.

Approach

Studied Apple Human Interface Guidelines and Google Material Design 3 to understand platform conventions and user expectations. Designed separate but cohesive experiences for iOS and Android leveraging native navigation patterns (tab bars on iOS, bottom navigation on Android), interaction gestures, and visual conventions specific to each platform.

Optimized for touch interactions with appropriate touch targets (minimum 44x44px), gesture support (swipe, long-press, pinch-zoom), and mobile-specific constraints like smaller screens and variable connectivity. Tested across multiple device sizes including phones and tablets, and iOS/Android versions.

Key Deliverables

  • iOS native application following HIG with tab-based navigation
  • Android native application following Material Design 3
  • Platform-specific gesture interactions and animations
  • Responsive layouts supporting phones (5.5" to 6.7") and tablets
  • Native mobile components (pickers, date selectors, bottom sheets)
  • Mobile-specific onboarding and permission request flows
  • Offline capability and progressive disclosure of features

Outcomes & Impact

  • iOS app rating: 4.9/5 stars, Android app rating: 4.7/5 stars (4.8 average)
  • Mobile usage increased 45% post-launch
  • User retention improved 30% for mobile-first users
  • Mobile feature adoption rates consistently above 70%
  • Reduced crash reports and support issues related to mobile experience
  • Mobile revenue contributed 35% of platform total by year 2

Key Learnings

Platform-native experiences matter—users notice when apps don't follow platform conventions. Touch optimization is critical for mobile usability. Testing on actual devices with various OS versions reveals issues that desktop testing misses. Mobile isn't just a smaller version of desktop—it requires rethinking workflows and information architecture for mobile contexts.

WCAG 2.1 AA Accessibility Implementation

Standard: WCAG 2.1 AA
Coverage: 100% Compliance
Platforms: Web and Mobile
Focus: Inclusive Design

Challenge

Creating truly accessible digital interfaces requires understanding diverse user needs and disabilities including visual impairments (blindness, low vision, color blindness), hearing impairments, motor impairments, and cognitive disabilities. Accessibility standards are detailed and technical. Many designers treat accessibility as a compliance checkbox rather than a design principle.

Approach

Embedded accessibility thinking into design process from start, not as final review step. For each design, I considered: color contrast for users with color blindness or low vision, keyboard navigation for users with motor impairments, semantic structure for users relying on screen readers, appropriate focus indicators for keyboard users, and clear error messages for all users.

Conducted accessibility audits using WCAG 2.1 AA checklist and tools like WAVE and Lighthouse. Tested with assistive technologies including screen readers (NVDA, JAWS) and keyboard-only navigation. Educated design and engineering teams on accessibility best practices through workshops and documentation.

Key Deliverables

  • Color palettes meeting WCAG AA color contrast ratios (4.5:1 for normal text)
  • Semantic HTML with proper heading hierarchy and landmark regions
  • Keyboard navigation support for all interactive elements
  • Focus indicators visible on all focusable elements
  • Form labels and instructions for accessibility with screen readers
  • Error messages clear and visible with visual and textual indicators
  • Accessible data visualizations and complex UI patterns
  • Accessibility documentation and team training materials

Outcomes & Impact

  • Achieved 100% WCAG 2.1 AA compliance across all platforms
  • Expanded addressable user base by making products accessible to users with disabilities
  • Improved usability for all users (good accessibility often improves general usability)
  • Eliminated legal/regulatory risk related to accessibility non-compliance
  • Established accessibility culture where teams proactively consider accessibility
  • Reduced support issues related to accessibility concerns
  • Improved team skill and awareness around inclusive design

Key Learnings

Accessibility is not an afterthought or compliance burden—it's integral to good design. Accessible design benefits everyone, not just users with disabilities (captions help users in noisy environments, keyboard navigation helps power users, clear information hierarchy helps all users understand complex interfaces). Accessibility requires understanding real user needs, not just following checklist. Team education is essential—accessibility responsibility extends beyond designers to product managers and engineers.
